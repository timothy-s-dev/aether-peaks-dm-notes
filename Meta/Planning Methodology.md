# Prep Situations, Not Plots
While I have a general idea of what the party is going to be doing for the first "arc" (attempting to complete the Pilgrimage of Ember) I want to avoid pushing or forcing them into certain paths or actions.

Generally, I want to lean into the idea of prepping situations, not plots. I want minimal specifics about *what* happens, but a decent amount of information about the state of things as the PCs enter the proverbial scene. I expect to make an exception to this rule for the introductory adventure, just to set things off on a good track.

# Meaningful Decisions
An adventure should always provide the players with a sequence of Meaningful Decisions.  For a decision to be "meaningful", it needs to meet the following criteria:
* The decision will have an impact in the future that the PCs will observe (and ideally benefit from or be penalized by)
	* DO: Rescuing Vinn Berghish both gives the PCs almost immediate information about what went wrong with their trolley line, and also gives them an ally going forward
	* DON'T: You can pick between these two roads, and one will technically take longer, but that extra time won't have any impact on the story.
* The PCs are able to make the decision based on accurate information.
	* DO: There are two roads you could take; the first would give you a better chance to find your quarry, but it will take you longer to reach your destination and I'll tick off a Grim Portent; the other will get you there in time to stop XYZ, but you'll almost certainly miss your ally and have to deal with it alone.
	* DON'T: There are two roads, which one do you take?
* There should not be a "right" or "wrong" answer (then it's just math, shouldn't be an exercise in optimization)
	* DO: See above road example
	* DON'T: There are two roads you could take, one will take longer and is more dangerous, the other is quicker and safer.

For any scene/adventure I want to minimize/gloss over decisions and events that aren't "meaningful" by this definition as much as possible, and get to the opportunities for the PCs to make interesting, meaningful choices.

# Fronts
I am planning to use the "Fronts" system from Dungeon World to keep track of the activities of the various "bad guy" factions (and maybe sometimes for the "good" ones).  Both at a campaign level, and an adventure level.

A Front has 2-3 dangers.  Each danger has a name, description, 4+ Grim Portents, an Impending Doom, and 1-3 Stakes.  The Grim Portents are the "steps" in the "plan" for that danger - they are things that will come to pass if the party fails to interfere (either through inaction, or failed action). The Impending Doom is what happens if all the grim portents happen.  Stakes are questions about people, places, or groups we want to answer through play. They should be concrete/clear, not about vague feelings or incremental changes.

Ideally, stopping a single Grim Portent shouldn't totally stop the Danger as a whole, but should have a meaningful impact on their plans.  A good Grim Portent should be something the party will have an opportunity to see, and maybe stop depending on their actions.  If it's just background stuff it's set-dressing, not a Grim Portent.

More details on Fronts can be found here: https://www.dungeonworldsrd.com/gamemastering#TOC-Fronts

# Good, Bad, and Ugly Factions
I am planning to make use of a "Good, Bad, and Ugly" design pattern for factions.  E.G. For a given situation I want three factions, a "good" faction that the party is likely to align with, a "bad" faction opposing them, and an "ugly" faction that is more gray/complex and throws a wrench into the mix.  The idea is that without the "ugly" faction, things are at a bit of an impasse, and the party interfering (potentially bringing the "ugly" faction in on one side or the other) will shift the balance.

This idea can be applied recursively to whatever depth is useful.  Within a faction I may have 3 sub-factions.  E.G. In the Empire (the "good" faction) there might be three sub-factions (there are far more, but I want to limit how many are relevant to the story at a time): The Ember Guard (good), The Imperial Church (bad), and the Guiding Counsel (ugly).  Within the Ember Guard, I might have three key NPCs (obviously more may exist): A handler/contact for the party (good), a planted Talon spy (bad), and a rival group of Ember Guard (ugly).

# PC Character Exploration in Early Adventures
For early adventures I want to mix in periodic character questions. E.G. When a PC first receives their letter naming them an Aspirant to the Ember Guard, I might ask what their character's first thought is, or who/what they're leaving behind when they go.  I also want to periodically invite them to invent details for the world beyond their character.

Part of this is to give me hooks for future adventures, but I'm hoping it also encourages a bit more player buy-in (for their character and the world at large) and more complex characters in the long run.

# In-Media-Res
When possible, I want to start each session with a bang (occasionally literally).  For the introductory adventure this means I intend to start with the cable-car the party is traveling on having its cable snap and begin falling against the side of the mountain.

For future sessions I want to try to do something similar - go straight into something important happening, whether that's a combat, a social encounter, or some sort of high-time-pressure skill check.

# Organizing Session Notes
When going into a session, I want to make sure I have the following at my finger-tips:
* Brief reminder of where we left off
* The plan for the In-Media-Res opening
* Faction dossiers (for factions likely to be relevant to the session)
	* This includes the info from the Fronts section
